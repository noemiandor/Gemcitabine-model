\subsection*{Methods}
\subsubsection*{Model 1}

Hypothesis 1: Cells continue to divide under treatment, but most of them die in the process. Over time resistance develops as dividing cells reduce their apoptotic risk.\newline


We use a log-kill pattern for modeling treatment effect, which assumes that the shrinkage rate of the population as a result of drug treatment is proportional to population size:


\begin{equation} \label{m1}
\frac{\mathrm{d} T}{\mathrm{~d} t}=f(T)-k_{\mathrm{d}} \cdot e^{-\lambda \cdot t} \cdot \text { Exposure } \cdot T
\end{equation}

The $e_{-\lambda \cdot t}$ term quantifies the decline of drug effect over time (i.e. resistance) and $f(T)$ is the growth term. For example, for logistic growth we have: $f(T) = r \cdot T \cdot (1 - \frac{T}{K})$


\subsubsection*{Model 2}

Hypothesis 2: Resistance is a function of how effectively tumor cells are able to enter (and exit) the polyploid state in response to a given drug concentration.\newline

When exposed to Gemcitabine, cells can move between different compartments ($P_i$) by endoreplicating ( $i \geq 0$ indicates how many mitoses they have skipped). The rate $k(i,x)$ at which cells endoreplicate depends on the drug concentration $x$ and the current ploidy state $i$:

\begin{equation}
k(i,x) = (v*x)^{i+1}
\end{equation}
 
The rate at which cells die, which also depends on drug concentration and ploidy state:

\begin{equation}
d(i,x) = (w*x)^{i+1}
\end{equation}

We can then model the change in the concentration of live cells of different ploidy ($P_i$) and dead cells ($D$) as:

\begin{subequations}\label{main}
\begin{align}
\diff{P_i}{t} = k(i-1,x) P_{i-1}  - k(i,x) P_{i} - d(i,x) P_i + \alpha(i,x)  P_i  \\
\diff{D}{t} = \sum_i d(i,x)P_i 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

, where the proliferation rate ($\alpha(i, x)$) also depends on ploidy state and drug concentration. Since in most cases the polyploid state is quiescent, proliferation rate will be 0 once cells have skipped a single mitotic event (i.e. when $i>0$). However a subset of cells manage to resume proliferation in the tetraploid state (i.e. for $i=1$). We model this by introducing the parameter $\iota$ as follows:

\begin{equation}
\alpha(i, x) = a \frac{1}{x} H( \iota - i)
\end{equation}

For our $2N$ cells we set $\iota=0$, whereas our tetraploidized cell lineage we set $\iota=1$, i.e. these cells can proliferate in the tetraploid state, but also ceise proliferation for even higher ploidy states.
