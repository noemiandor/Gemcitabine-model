\subsection*{Main}
Polyploid/ polyaneuploid giant cancer (PGCC/PACC) (PGCC henceforth) state is a common response of cancer cells to various stressors including chemotherapy, irradiation, hypoxia and viral infection. Upon stress, PGCCs adopt an endoreplication in which the genome replicates, mitosis is omitted, and cells grow in size, leading to resistance. How accessible endoreplication is to a cell, therefore, directly translates to its resistance to therapy. In this study, we hypothesized that endoreplication and PGCC state are more plausible to cancer cells that already have a higher ploidy content prior to therapy. To test this hypothesis, we developed a comprehensive three-tier framework consisting of i) computational, ii) in vitro and iii) in silico components.
We designed a set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to model how cells enter and exit the PGCC state in response to a given stressor. We engineered how this in silico approach interacts with in vitro experiments into a broadly applicable software solution called CLONEID. The software uses computer vision to monitor phenotypic changes in cell and nuclear size from standard bright-field microscopy and classify cells into PGCC and non-PGCC states. We used CLONEID to test various therapeutic agents for their ability to select for a stable near-tetraploid population in a set of near-diploid cell lines. Through spontaneous cell fusions, we also obtained tetraploid breast cancer cells matched with their parental lines. Altogether, this framework, now, enables us to monitor the PGCC state experimentally in matched cell lines with differential DNA content to model the successful entrance and exit rates to and from the endoreplication state, respectively.

\subsection*{Results}

As the first application of this framework, we tested the ability of our matched triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) lines (SUM-159 and MDA-MB-231) to access the PGCC state upon treatment with 18 commonly-used chemotherapy agents. We observed that gemcitabine caused continued cell growth without cell division in both tetraploid SUM-159 (Figure 1A-F) and MDA-MB-231 cells (Figure 2A) whereas near-diploid parental cells were hypersensitive to the treatment. Consequently, tetraploid cancer cells continued to safely grow in the presence of gemcitabine (Figure 3A). Furthermore, these PGCCs re-entered the proliferative cell cycle and grew in cell number when treatment is terminated (Figure 3B).

Based on these findings, we have formulated an in silico model of the interactions of cancer cells, ploidy level and drug response as follows. Let C and P be the compartments of cycling and polyploid cells respectively. Then we can model their temporal dynamics when exposed to x units of a drug (i.e. ploidy-modulating agent) as shown in equations (1) and (2) in Figure 3.

 Cycling and polyploid cells die at different rates (d\_C and d\_P respectively), which are both a function of drug concentration x (Figure 3). Hereby, we assume that cells cease dividing under treatment and that resistance is a function of how effectively tumor cells can enter and exit the polyploid state in response to a given drug concentration. The model can easily be expanded to also account for multiple rounds of polyploidization and for potential depolyploidization after treatment is terminated.

Gemcitabine (GEM)-based chemotherapy is a standard treatment for patients with TNBC although its efficacy is limited mainly due to drug resistance. Moreover, such poor response rate is coupled with severe side effects that frequently leads to the failure of vital organs, serious secondary diseases, and co-morbidities. Our findings suggest that ploidy is a predictive biomarker for gemcitabine sensitivity. Thus, we expect our findings and three-component framework strategy to help stratify the TNBC patient population by their response to gemcitabine. In addition, our mathematical modelling approach has the promising potential to inform personalized dose optimization and to effectively decrease administered gemcitabine dose for a subset of patients, which would alleviate therapy-associated side effects and lethalities.

We assigned each live cells to a ploidy state $i$, defined by the number of mitoses skipped ($i \in \{0,1,2\}$). For the classification, we trained a regression model as follows:

\[ i ~ \sim \xi \times \theta_{100} \times \eta_{40} \times \eta_{100} \times \eta_{60} \times time \]

, where $\xi$ is the nucleus area of a cell and $\theta_x$ and $\eta_x$ are the cumulative area occupied by all live or dead cells respectively, in a local neighborhood of x $\mu$m. Cell density varies spatially and is known to affect cell and nucleus size \cite{mugahid_yap_2020}, which in turn is the main proxy of ploidy in the data. $\theta_x$ and $\eta_x$ account for this effect -- even though nucleus area decreases with cell density, the relative difference in nucleus size between 2N and 4N cells is maintained across various cell density bins (Supplementary Fig. \ref{SI_Ploidy_Area_Density}). 

We trained the model on data with available ploidy states: (i) 7,833 2N SUM-159 cells across all treatment conditions imaged during the first 16 hours after treatment exposure were assigned $i=0$ (i.e. they skipped zero mitotic events) and (ii) 6,298 4N SUM-159 cells across all treatment conditions imaged during the first 16 hours after treatment exposure were assigned $i=1$ (i.e. they skipped one mitotic event). Hereby we assumed that drug-induced polyploidization did not take place in the first 16 hours after Gemcitabine exposure. 

We obtained an adjusted R-squared of 0.21. We then applied the trained model on 2N and 4N SUM-159 cells treated with 31.25 nM Gemcitabine up until 5 days after initial exposure.



\subsubsection*{Mathematical model of polyploidization}

When exposed to Gemcitabine, cells can move between different compartments ($P_i$) by endoreplicating ( $i \geq 0$ indicates how many mitoses they have skipped). The rate at which cells endoreplicate ($k(i,x)$) or die ($d(i,x)$) depends on the drug concentration $x$ and the current ploidy state $i$. The rate at which cells proliferate also depends on drug concentration and ploidy state. Since in most cases the polyploid state is quiescent, proliferation rate will be 0 once cells have skipped a single mitotic event (i.e. when $i>0$). However some lineages have evolved to resume proliferation in the tetraploid state (i.e. for $i=1$). We model this by introducing the parameter $\iota$ as follows:

\begin{equation}
\alpha(i, x) =  H( \iota - i) (1 - \frac{{x}}{{x + a}})
\end{equation}

For our $2N$ cells we set $\iota=0$, whereas for our tetraploidized cell lineage we set $\iota=1$, i.e. these cells can proliferate in the tetraploid state, but they too cease proliferation for higher ploidy states $i > 1$.

We assume all three rates, (cell death-, proliferation- and endoreplication rates), follow Michaelis-Menten kinetics as a function of drug concentration, with Michaelis constants $w, a, v$ respectively.

With this we can model the change in the concentration of live cells of different ploidy ($P_i$) and dead cells ($A$) as:

\begin{subequations}\label{main}
\begin{align}
\diff{P_i}{t} = \alpha(i, x) P_i + k(i-1,x) P_{i-1} - k(i, x) P_i - d(i, x) P_i \\
\diff{A}{t} = \sum_i d(i,x)P_i 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\subsubsection*{Optimization routine}

We observed a higher resistance to Gemictabine-exposure among 4N- compared to 2N SUM-159 lineages (Fig. XX), further denoted as $T$ and $D$ cells respectively. Because both $T$ cells and the few $D$ cells that did survive exposure, were enlarged in response to Gemcitabine, we hypothesized that $T$ cells were more efficient at entering the polyploid state, protecting them from Gemcitabine-induced death. To test this hypothesis we fit the model given by eq. \eqref{main} to the data, assuming a monotonically decreasing relationship between the number of skipped mitosis, $i$, and probability of cell death:

\begin{subequations}\label{death_rate}
\begin{align}
d(i, x) = \left(\frac{{K - i}}{{K}}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{{x}}{{x + w}}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

, where $K$ is the maximum number of skipped mitoses for the duration of the experiment. We tested various functional forms of the dependence of enrodreplication rate on number of skipped mitoses, including constant, monotonically decreasing, increasing and non-monotonic relationships: 

\begin{subequations}\label{endo_rate}
\begin{align}
k(i, x) =  \frac{{x}}{{x + v}} \\
k(i, x) =  \left(\frac{{K - i}}{{K}}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{{x}}{{x + v}} \\
k(i, x) =  \left(\frac{i}{{K}}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{{x}}{{x + v}} \\
k(i, x) = (1.05 - (1 - i)^2) \cdot \frac{{x}}{{x + v}} 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

For a given choice of the three parameters $a, w, v$, the goodness of fit is evaluated using the Wasserstein distance as a metric to measure the discrepancy between observed and simulated data. The evaluation process can be described as follows. First, ODEs are solved using an appropriate solver with the following configurations: (i) For the first case, the ODE solver is invoked with the parameters $a, w, v, \iota=0$, and the resulting solution vectors are denoted as $D$. (ii) For the second case, the ODE solver is invoked with the parameters $a, w, v, \iota=1$, and the resulting solution vectors are denoted as $T$.

Let $\hat D, \hat T$ be the measurements corresponding to simulations $D, T$ respectively. The overall discrepancy between the simulated and observed data is then calculated as:
\[
J = \frac{1}{2K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \text{wsd}(D_i, \hat D_i) + \text{wsd}(T_i, \hat T_i)
\]
where $\text{wsd}(Y, \hat Y)$ calculates the Wasserstein distance between the simulated and observed data. This equation represents the combined discrepancy between the simulated data for both cases (N2 and N4) and their corresponding observed data, providing a quantitative measure of the goodness of fit for the given parameter values $a, w, v$.


\subsubsection*{Modeling Results}

Simulations that best recapitulated the measurements (Fig. \ref{ODE_model}A,B), made the assumption of: (i) a monotonically decreasing relationship between the number of skipped mitosis and probability of cell death (Fig. \ref{ODE_model}D; eq. \eqref{death_rate}) and (ii) a non-monotonic relation  between number of skipped mitoses and endoreplication rate (eq. \eqref{endo_rate}d). Hereby the rate of a subsequent endoreplication was maximal, when cells have skipped exactly $i:=1$ mitoses (Fig. \ref{ODE_model}E).

Allowing any of the three Michaelis-constants ($a, w, v$) to vary between the two lineages did not result in a better fit and the fitted values were similar across replicates (Fig. \ref{ODE_model}F). This suggests that 4N cells are more resistant to Gemcitabine than 2N cells because the former have already skipped a mitotic event prior to drug-exposure, rather than other intrinsic differences between the two lineages.
